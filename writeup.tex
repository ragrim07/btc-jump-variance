{\LARGE\bfseries Self-Exciting Jump Dynamics in Bitcoin Variance}\\[3pt]
{\large\color{muted} Do jump clusters predict when the variance risk premium compresses?}\\[8pt]
{\color{muted}\small 1{,}838{,}880 one-minute bars, Jan 2023 -- Jun 2026 \quad$\cdot$\quad
Deribit DVOL options data \quad$\cdot$\quad all code and derivations in repository}

\vspace{6pt}\hrule\vspace{8pt}

\textbf{The question.} Bitcoin's price jumps arrive in clusters, like earthquakes
and their aftershocks. If a cluster is running, is the options market's variance
premium about to compress? Answering that needs a latent quantity nobody
observes --- the \emph{jump-variance state} --- so the project builds the
machinery to extract it, verifies that machinery works, and then tests the
trading question honestly.

\section{What I found}

\textbf{1. Bitcoin jumps cluster, and the standard model cannot accommodate it.}
The textbook constant-intensity jump-diffusion predicts that jump energy on
disjoint days is independent: the $\JV$ autocovariance is \emph{exactly zero at
every lag, for every parameter value}. It is not.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
model-free test & standard model predicts & data\\
\midrule
Jump clustering, $\mathrm{Cov}(\JV_t,\JV_{t-k})$, joint over 10 lags & $0$ & $t = \mathbf{3.05}$\\
Diffusive variance leading future jumps, joint over 5 lags & $0$ & $t = \mathbf{2.78}$\\
Lag-1 jump clustering alone & $0$ & $t = \mathbf{3.74}$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{FIG1_finding.png}
\end{center}

\textbf{2. A self-exciting (Hawkes) repair fits.} Letting the jump arrival rate
depend on the jump-variance state, $\lambda(t)=\lambda_0+\lambda_1 V^j(t^-)$,
reproduces both curves. Calibrated by GMM on 40 moments: only \textbf{2 of 40}
moments miss by more than two standard errors --- what chance alone produces ---
and the misses are scattered rather than concentrated in any block. Point
estimates: about \textbf{24\%} of jumps are aftershocks of earlier jumps
($\hat\eta = 0.24$), individual shocks decay with a 1.8-day half-life while
\emph{clusters} persist 2.4 days.

\textbf{3. Bitcoin variance carries a large risk premium.} Option-implied
variance exceeds the model's physical expectation by \textbf{20\% on average} and
on \textbf{65\% of days}; it exceeds subsequently realised variance by
\textbf{25\%}. The premium mean-reverts with a \textbf{13.8-day half-life}.

\textbf{4. The model still under-states persistence.} On held-out crash events,
variance stays elevated for \textbf{9.5 days} against the model's predicted
\textbf{2.4}. An independent over-identification test points the same way (a
$6.3\times$ gap), so something slow is still missing --- most likely a third
variance factor. The Hawkes repair moves the right direction but not far enough.

\section{How I know the machinery is right}

Before touching real data, the estimator was proved on data whose answers were
known. An exact simulator (Ogata thinning for self-exciting jumps, noncentral
$\chi^2$ for the CIR factor, analytic within-day integrals --- no discretisation)
generated $\ge$50{,}000 days at six known parameter vectors.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{FIG2_verification.png}
\end{center}

Every analytic moment landed inside Monte-Carlo error (max $|z| = 2.64$, none of
240 beyond $\pm3$); GMM recovered the true parameters; and 95\% bootstrap
intervals covered at 0.86--0.98 across 50 independent datasets.

\textbf{This process caught four real bugs}, each of which would have silently
corrupted the results:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.4em]
\item Tripower variation used exponent $4/3$ (quarticity) rather than $2/3$
(variance) --- the ``jump variance'' series was really \emph{total} variance.
Caught by cross-checking against a second robust estimator (MedRV), which now
agrees at $\rho = 0.996$.
\item The block bootstrap re-sliced the raw series and recomputed
autocovariances, splicing unrelated days together. Interval coverage for
$\kappa_c$ was $0.00$; resampling moment-contribution rows instead restored it to
$0.93$.
\item Shrinking the GMM weighting matrix toward a scaled identity ignored that
moment variances span five orders of magnitude, starving the jump-clustering
moments of weight. It had inverted the persistence estimate ($\kappa_j = 3.43$
versus the correct $0.29$).
\item One of the two over-identifying ``tests'' was an algebraic tautology of the
parametrisation --- it holds for arbitrary parameters and tests nothing. It only
has content when the coefficients are estimated freely from separate moment
blocks, which is how it now runs.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The trading question, and the honest answer}

Sorting every day by how rich the premium is \emph{and} how hot the jump state
is produces a large, ordered in-sample effect. Holding the premium rich, a hot
jump state compresses it roughly $20\times$ harder than a quiet one over the
following ten days, and raises the odds of a large compression by $2.1\times$.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{FIG3_honest_limit.png}
\end{center}

\textbf{It does not replicate out of sample.} On one year of held-out data the
ordering breaks, and the single cell that would support the effect contains eight
observations. With roughly 35 independent ten-day windows, the holdout can
neither confirm nor refute --- but it certainly did not confirm.

Three further results were discarded once tested properly, each an artifact
rather than a signal:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=1.4em]
\item The premium appeared to predict variance-selling returns at $t = 5.7$ ---
but signal and payoff share the implied-variance leg ($\rho = 0.97$), and the
effect vanishes once that is controlled ($t = 0.99$).
\item The continuous-variance state appeared to predict premium changes at
$t = 2.5$ --- but the model's forecast is built \emph{from} that state, a purely
mechanical channel ($t = 8.5$). Against pure market data the effect is
$t = 0.10$.
\item The structural forecast does not beat a three-term HAR regression
($R^2$ 0.117 versus 0.113).
\end{itemize}

\section{Method, briefly}

Two latent factors drive total variance: a persistent Brownian-driven CIR
component and a jump-driven component whose arrival intensity is affine in its
own level, making jumps self-exciting (equivalent to a marked Hawkes process).
Because the intensity is affine, all 40 unconditional moments --- means,
variances, autocovariances and two-sided cross-covariances of daily tripower
variation and jump variance --- remain in closed form, and are matched to their
sample analogues by GMM with an analytic $40\times9$ Jacobian, Ledoit--Wolf
regularised weighting, and moving-block bootstrap intervals. Parameters are
estimated on 2023-01 to 2025-06 only; 2025-07 onward, containing the October 2025
crash, is never used in estimation.

\section{What I would do next}

The persistence shortfall (9.5 days observed against 2.4 predicted) and the
$6.3\times$ over-identification gap point at the same omission, so the natural
extension is a third, slower variance factor. Separately, the compression result
needs more held-out data than one year to be settled; the sample, not the method,
is the binding constraint.
